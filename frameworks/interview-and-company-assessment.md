# Interview preparation and company assessment

## Purpose

Help a candidate prepare to demonstrate their value while gathering enough evidence to decide whether the company and role are worth joining.

The interview is a two-way diligence process:

- the company assesses the candidate's ability to succeed in the role;
- the candidate assesses the company's purpose, leadership and operating conditions;
- the product distinguishes claims, examples, interpretations, contradictions and unknowns;
- personal hard gates are applied before an overall recommendation.

The company-assessment model is derived from [Seven pillars of a thriving software company](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1SxCTacvC5ZOHvM9pU-a5C5DwmrynWCffVgh2ShUrLKA/edit).

## The seven pillars

### 1. Meaningful purpose and customer impact

**Intended experience:** I understand who we help and why my work matters.

**Ask:**

- What customer problem would this role help solve in its first six months?
- How does the team learn whether shipped work improved the customer's experience?
- Tell me about something the team stopped or changed because customer evidence did not support it.

**Healthy evidence:** Teams receive customer feedback, outcomes are defined beyond feature output, and learning can change priorities.

**Warning evidence:** Mission language is disconnected from daily work, or success is described mainly through deadlines and feature volume.

### 2. Clear direction and priorities

**Intended experience:** I know what matters now, what success means and who decides.

**Ask:**

- What are the most important outcomes for this role in the first 90 days, and what is explicitly outside its scope?
- How many major priorities would I normally own at once?
- Tell me about the last urgent priority change. Who decided, how was it explained, and what work stopped?

**Healthy evidence:** Few priorities, explicit decision authority, visible trade-offs and stable definitions of success.

**Warning evidence:** Everything is urgent, instructions conflict, or responsibility is assigned without decision authority.

### 3. Autonomy with support

**Intended experience:** I can make decisions within my responsibility and get help when needed.

**Ask:**

- Which decisions would I own, and which require agreement from others?
- Tell me about a recent decision a team made differently from what its manager initially expected.
- When someone is blocked or a decision becomes risky, what support and escalation routes exist?

**Healthy evidence:** Outcomes and constraints are clear, teams choose solutions, and coaching and escalation routes are available.

**Warning evidence:** Micromanagement, unclear decision boundaries, or accountability without access to information, resources or support.

### 4. Trust and psychological safety

**Intended experience:** I can ask questions, disagree and report mistakes without humiliation.

**Ask:**

- Tell me about a recent disagreement with a senior leader. What happened next?
- What happens when someone reports bad news or makes a significant mistake?
- When did feedback from the team last cause a leader to change their approach?

**Healthy evidence:** Leaders acknowledge uncertainty, invite challenge and use mistakes to improve systems while maintaining responsible expectations.

**Warning evidence:** People who raise concerns are punished, disrespect from high performers is tolerated, or disagreement is treated as disloyalty.

### 5. Growth and competence

**Intended experience:** I am getting better and can see a future here.

**Ask:**

- How are expectations for progression made clear in this role?
- What development happened during working time for someone on the team recently?
- How frequently do people receive useful feedback outside formal performance reviews?

**Healthy evidence:** Specific feedback, mentoring, practice opportunities, understandable progression and planned learning time.

**Warning evidence:** Vague promotion criteria, annual-review-only feedback, or development expected entirely outside work.

### 6. Sustainable working conditions

**Intended experience:** I have the time, tools and energy to do good work.

**Ask:**

- How often has urgent work displaced planned work in the last three months?
- What overtime, after-hours work or incident response has the team experienced in the last six months?
- How much of a normal week is recurring meetings, and how is focus time protected?
- What recurring friction or unreliable tooling is the team currently trying to improve?

**Healthy evidence:** Realistic workload planning, protected focus, explicit capacity trade-offs, maintained tools and bounded operational responsibility.

**Warning evidence:** Chronic overtime, repeated emergencies, unreliable tools, excessive work in progress, or productivity measured mainly through activity counts.

### 7. Fairness, recognition and belonging

**Intended experience:** My contribution is valued and decisions affecting me are fair.

**Ask:**

- How are pay, performance and promotion decisions explained?
- How are mentoring, maintenance, operational improvement and teamwork recognised alongside visible launches?
- Tell me about a recent decision where the company actively sought views from people outside the usual leadership group.

**Healthy evidence:** Explainable standards, consistent decisions, recognition of less-visible contributions and participation across roles.

**Warning evidence:** Favouritism, invisible work going unrecognised, inconsistent standards, or belonging depending on similarity to leadership.

## Interview workflow

### 1. Prepare

Create one opportunity-specific interview pack containing:

- the role's required outcomes and likely interview topics;
- the candidate's strongest verified evidence for each topic;
- concise situation-action-outcome stories, with unsupported claims excluded;
- likely difficult questions and truthful gap explanations;
- company claims that need verification;
- personal hard gates and unresolved risks;
- three to five candidate questions appropriate to the interview stage.

### 2. Rehearse

Use interactive practice rather than a static answer sheet:

- ask one realistic question at a time;
- allow the candidate to answer in their own words;
- assess relevance, evidence, clarity and seniority signal;
- identify missing context or unsupported claims;
- help tighten the answer without replacing the candidate's voice;
- practise follow-up questions and interruptions.

### 3. Investigate during interviews

Select questions according to who can answer them credibly:

| Interview stage | Primary evidence to seek |
|---|---|
| Recruiter | Compensation, location, process, formal flexibility, role history and obvious hard gates |
| Hiring manager | Outcomes, priorities, decision rights, workload, interruptions, support and leadership behaviour |
| Future peer | Ordinary working week, psychological safety, meetings, operational load, collaboration and informal norms |
| Senior leader or final interview | Customer purpose, strategic trade-offs, fairness, organisational incentives and whether leaders model stated values |

Do not ask every question. Prefer recent behavioural examples over adjectives such as *supportive*, *autonomous* or *fast-paced*.

### 4. Debrief immediately

For every material observation, capture:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pillar | Which of the seven dimensions it informs |
| Claim | What the interviewer or company stated |
| Example | The concrete event, practice, number or artefact offered as support |
| Source | Who provided it and at which interview stage |
| Interpretation | What the candidate thinks it suggests |
| Confidence | Strong evidence, partial evidence, weak signal or unknown |
| Contradiction | Any conflict with another answer, advert or observed behaviour |
| Follow-up | What still needs to be verified and who can answer it |

The debrief should also record how the interview process itself behaved. Repeated rescheduling, unclear ownership, disrespectful questioning, inconsistent role descriptions or pressure to ignore agreed boundaries are relevant evidence, not merely administrative noise.

### 5. Decide

Apply decision logic in this order:

1. **Personal hard gates:** compensation, location, working pattern, on-call, ethical boundaries and other non-negotiables.
2. **Serious pillar concerns:** evidence of chronic overload, unsafe leadership, unfair treatment or structurally unclear accountability cannot be cancelled out by strengths elsewhere.
3. **Pillar evidence profile:** show each pillar as healthy evidence, mixed evidence, concerning evidence or unknown.
4. **Role fit:** assess whether the work uses the candidate's strengths and supports the desired career direction.
5. **Recommendation:** proceed, proceed with explicit verification, pause, or withdraw.

Unknown is not positive evidence. A polished answer without a concrete example remains unknown or a weak signal.

### 6. Learn

After each process, update:

- the candidate's interview story bank;
- questions that produced useful evidence;
- recurring company risks and positive signals;
- recruiter and market intelligence;
- gaps in the candidate's evidence or interview communication;
- the final outcome and any later evidence about whether the assessment was accurate.

## Product safeguards

- Never invent candidate experience, company evidence or interview answers.
- Keep company statements separate from candidate interpretations.
- Preserve unknowns and contradictions instead of forcing certainty.
- Do not infer healthy working conditions from benefits, branding or culture adjectives.
- Do not use protected personal characteristics to score companies or candidates.
- Do not record or transcribe an interview without appropriate participant consent.
- Do not optimise only for receiving an offer; optimise for a good mutual decision.
